Synthetic tabular data generation has moved from classical parametric models and minority oversampling toward deep generative frameworks that approximate high-dimensional, mixed-type joint distributions under competing utility and privacy pressures. Two design decisions recur and largely determine suitability for biomedical data: the \emph{generative backbone} that models the joint distribution, and the \emph{control mechanism} that governs validity, class coverage, and disclosure risk. We organize prior work along these axes, since high-fidelity clinical synthesis depends less on any single backbone than on how validity is enforced on top of it (Table~\ref{tab:relatedwork_comparison}).

\subsection{Generative Backbones}
The dominant backbones trade off in complementary ways, and none resolves the clinical setting on its own. Adversarial and autoencoding models were the first to handle heterogeneous columns: CTGAN~\cite{xu2019modeling} introduced mode-specific normalization and conditional sampling, MedGAN~\cite{choi2017generating} paired an autoencoder with adversarial training for high-dimensional diagnostic codes, and the more recent TAEGAN~\cite{li2025taegangeneratingsynthetictabular} uses a masked autoencoder generator to stabilize training. These methods are fast and often reach high utility on balanced data, but the discrete, non-differentiable nature of tabular features makes them prone to mode collapse, so rare yet clinically decisive subpopulations are systematically underrepresented.

Diffusion models shift the emphasis toward distributional fidelity. TabDDPM~\cite{kotelnikov2023tabddpm} and the latent-space TabSyn~\cite{tabsyn} model data through iterative denoising and report strong marginal fidelity together with favorable proximity-based privacy measures. The same Gaussian-noise mechanism, however, tends to blur sharp clinical thresholds and rare co-morbidity structure; as our privacy analysis shows (Section~\ref{sec:privacyResults}), the resulting separation from real records often reflects noise that pushes samples off the valid clinical manifold rather than meaningful protection, and downstream utility suffers accordingly. A related limitation is that TabSyn models the joint latent \emph{unconditionally}, so on small or imbalanced datasets it can drop the minority class entirely; we observe this collapse on eleven of our benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), a failure mode that is especially consequential in clinical data where the minority class is usually the event of interest.

Autoregressive Transformers reframe a record as a token sequence and inherit the semantic priors of pretraining. Fine-tuned generators such as GReaT~\cite{borisov2022language}, RealTabFormer~\cite{solatorio2023realtabformer}, and HARMONIC~\cite{Wang2024HARMONICHL} capture global distributions and rare categorical values more reliably than earlier architectures, while prompt-based approaches such as SynLLM~\cite{ilaty2025synllm} and AIGT~\cite{zhang2024aigtaigenerativetable} steer frozen models through context engineering, and TabularARGN~\cite{sidorenko2025tabularargn} targets efficiency through randomized-permutation conditional generation. Their shared weakness is the objective: likelihood maximization rewards locally fluent text, so these models can emit records that are statistically plausible yet semantically invalid, such as a reported pregnancy for a male patient, with no explicit mechanism to detect or correct such violations during generation.

\subsection{Control: Constraints and Feedback}
A second line of work adds validity control on top of a backbone. In the database community, Kamino~\cite{ge2021kamino} and PrivMRF~\cite{cai2021datasynthesis} synthesize data while enforcing integrity constraints, but their rule-based formulations are rigid and hard to scale to high-dimensional neural generators. Neural feedback methods are closer to our setting: TAGAL~\cite{ronval2025tagal} runs an agentic loop in which a language model critiques its own outputs through natural-language prompts, and P-TA~\cite{yang2025ptausingproximalpolicy} applies Proximal Policy Optimization with a single binary discriminator. Both demonstrate that feedback can improve generation, yet their signal is either textual or a lone scalar, so it cannot indicate \emph{where} a record fails, for example that its syntax is valid but a diagnosis contradicts the recorded age. This inability to localize validity across semantic levels is the central shortcoming our design addresses.

\subsection{Privacy Evaluation and Attacks}
Synthetic data is not private by construction: generators can leak training records through memorization or distributional collisions. TableGAN-MCA~\cite{hu2021tableganmca} formalizes a membership-collision threat in which black-box access to synthetic samples partially recovers training records even when standard membership inference appears weak, motivating privacy auditing beyond utility metrics. Where formal guarantees are required, differentially private mechanisms such as PATE-GAN~\cite{jordon2019pategan} bound disclosure through private aggregation of teacher discriminators, at a cost in fidelity. These two strands frame privacy as a spectrum from empirical auditing to mathematically bounded protection, a distinction we adopt explicitly rather than conflating the two.

\subsection{Research Gap and Positioning}
Two gaps matter most for biomedical synthesis. First, validity is enforced, if at all, through a single objective, an adversarial loss, a likelihood, or one scalar reward, that cannot separately regulate syntactic, semantic, and statistical correctness. Second, minority-class coverage is treated as a byproduct of distribution matching, so rare clinical cohorts are dropped exactly where precision medicine needs them, and privacy is reported in isolation from utility. DISCO-TAB targets both: it decomposes the learning signal into discriminators at token, sentence, feature, and row granularity, couples them to reinforcement-learning fine-tuning, and adds inverse-frequency reward shaping to incentivize faithful minority generation. Its disclosure-risk evaluation is empirical and proximity-based, complementing rather than replacing differentially private synthesis. Existing feedback-driven methods (Table~\ref{tab:relatedwork_comparison}) already add control, but only through textual or single-scalar feedback; the multi-granular, reward-shaped formulation is what distinguishes our approach.
