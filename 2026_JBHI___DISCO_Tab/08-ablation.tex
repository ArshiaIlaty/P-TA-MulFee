To isolate what each component contributes, we remove one at a time and score the result on the metric that component is designed to govern, rather than on downstream utility alone. We ablate on three datasets spanning the regimes of interest, a tiny clinical cohort (Heart Failure), an imbalanced multi-class set (Obesity), and a large set (Diabetes), and report the mean in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}. Reading the ablation through downstream macro-$F_1$ alone is misleading: the random-forest label boundary on these datasets is largely recovered by supervised fine-tuning, so utility is close to saturated before the hierarchy acts. The components earn their place on the quality of the synthetic distribution, which is the generator's actual product.

\noindent\textbf{Indistinguishability from real data.}
The clearest signal is the classifier two-sample test (C2ST), which trains a discriminator to separate synthetic from real records: $0.5$ means indistinguishable and $1.0$ means trivially separable. The full system reaches $0.77$, whereas removing \emph{any} single component sends C2ST to $0.985$--$0.993$, i.e.\ the synthetic data becomes almost perfectly detectable. No single piece is responsible; the hierarchy only produces realistic records when its parts act together. This is the strongest evidence that the multi-granular design is necessary rather than incidental.

\noindent\textbf{Distributional and dependency fidelity.}
The full system also attains the lowest Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic ($0.100$ versus $0.16$--$0.21$ for every ablation) and the highest correlation preservation ($0.950$ versus $\leq 0.941$). The token- and sentence-level discriminators contribute most to marginal fidelity (removing either raises KS by roughly a factor of two), while the feature-level discriminator is the main driver of preserved cross-feature correlation, consistent with the role each was given in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.

\noindent\textbf{The utility trade-off.}
On raw macro-$F_1$, removing the reinforcement-learning stage or the inverse-frequency reward shaping slightly \emph{increases} the score ($0.86$ versus $0.81$), because fine-tuning alone already fits the label boundary on these datasets. That apparent gain is purchased at a steep cost in realism: the same ablated models are the most detectable (C2ST $\geq 0.99$) and the least faithful (highest KS). In other words, RL and reward shaping trade a marginal, dataset-specific change in downstream accuracy for synthetic data that is substantially closer to the real distribution and far harder to distinguish from it. For a generator whose purpose is to stand in for real clinical data, that is the trade we want, and it is why the full hierarchy is the default. These observations inform the discussion in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
